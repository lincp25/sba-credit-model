An expected loss model for SBA 7(a) small business loans

A bank has to price a loan before it knows how the loan turns out. This model does that from the record of 1,391,586 SBA-guaranteed loans approved between FY2000 and FY2026. Probability of default is a discrete-time hazard model with regime-specific coefficients; loss severity is measured from realised charge-offs. The two combine into an expected loss for each of 5,040 borrower profiles, which is then set against the rates lenders actually charged and against what the loan costs a bank to make.

Underlying data

The riskiest year is not the first one

Life-table default rates straight from the loan record, with no model applied. The conventional expectation is that a loan is riskiest when new and then seasons out. These loans do season out, but not for seven to ten years, and larger loans turn later than small ones. Each vintage window covers a different span, from 26 years for the 2000–2004 cohort down to 13 for 2013–2016.

Charge-offs are recorded with an administrative lag, so observed peaks sit later than the underlying defaults occurred. The shape is reliable; the exact month is not.

How much guarantee does this borrower need?

The SBA does not lend. It guarantees a share of each loan, absorbing that share of the loss and cutting the capital a lender has to hold. So every profile has a minimum guarantee at which the observed rate covers cost. For 472 of the 764 profiles with 100 or more loans and a recorded rate, that minimum is zero.

Limitations

What this cannot tell you

  • Charge-offs are recorded with an administrative lag. Defaults are dated later than they occurred, shifting the seasoning curve right by an unknown amount.
  • Interest rates appear in the SBA record only from FY2009. Pricing results rest on the post-2009 book, not the full twenty-six years.
  • Loss is measured against the original loan amount rather than the balance outstanding at default, overstating severity on long-seasoned loans.
  • The data covers approved loans only, with nothing on applicants who were declined. That is the population an access-to-capital argument most needs.

Two modelling choices are not implied by the data. Five-year expected loss is annualised by dividing by five, though realised losses are back-loaded. Capital is charged only on the unguaranteed portion, on the basis that a government guarantee carries a zero risk weight. The macroeconomic coefficients on the calculator sheet are hardcoded constants rather than estimated parameters, and apply only under the through-cycle regime.
