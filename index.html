An expected loss model for SBA 7(a) small business loans

SBA 7(a) loans are generally regarded as risky lending, and in aggregate the record bears this out. About 12% of loans default within five years, and a defaulted loan loses roughly 64% of its principal. The averages conceal a very wide spread. Loans under $50,000 default at 19.5% over five years while loans above $1M default at 1.7%, and severity falls from 83% to 57% across the same range. The programme therefore contains both marginal credit and lending a bank would compete for.

This model makes the spread visible at the level of an individual borrower rather than the book as a whole. Drawing on 1,391,586 loans approved between 2000 and 2026, the probability of default is modelled as a discrete-time hazard fitted to loan age, with separate coefficients for benign, average and crisis conditions, while the share of principal lost once a default occurs is measured directly from realised charge-offs. The two combine into an expected loss for each of 5,040 borrower profiles.

Lending at these loss rates requires protection, and the 7(a) programme is structured to provide it. The SBA does not itself lend. Private lenders originate and hold the loan, the SBA guarantees 75 to 85% of principal against loss, and the lender pays a fee for the guarantee and may sell the guaranteed portion into a secondary market. About half of loans are also secured against collateral. The model treats the guarantee and the collateral separately, since the record assigns them different roles. A guarantee reduces both the loss a lender bears and the capital it must hold, so it belongs on the cost side of the calculation. Collateral carries real information about whether a borrower defaults but almost none about how much is lost when one does, so it enters the default probability and is kept out of severity.

Setting the resulting expected loss against the rates lenders actually charged, and against what a loan costs a bank to originate and hold, separates the borrowers a bank can serve on commercial terms from the borrowers whose access depends on the guarantee.

The model suggests most SBA lending clears on commercial terms. At the rates lenders actually charged, 472 of 766 borrower profiles cover their full cost of capital with no guarantee applied, and those profiles carry 95% of the dollars lent. A further 237 cover cost only once the guarantee absorbs its share of the loss. Those 237 are a third of all loans and 5% of the dollars, so on this evidence the guarantee's marginal effect falls on the number of small businesses financed rather than on the volume of lending.

Life-table default rates straight from the loan record, with no model applied. The conventional expectation is that a loan is riskiest when new and then seasons out. These loans do season out, but not for seven to ten years, and larger loans turn later than small ones. A substantial share of these profiles is priced below cost. The following section examines how that shortfall is absorbed.

The guarantee

How much guarantee does this borrower need?

The SBA guarantees loans rather than making them. It absorbs a fixed share of each loss and cuts the capital a lender has to hold against the rest. Every profile therefore has a minimum guarantee at which the observed rate covers cost. For 472 of the 766 profiles with 100 or more loans and a recorded rate, that minimum is zero.

The preceding sections set out the argument. The limitations that qualify it follow.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Charge-offs are recorded with an administrative lag. Defaults are dated later than they occurred, shifting the seasoning curve right by an unknown amount.
  • Interest rates appear in the SBA record only from FY2009. Pricing results rest on the post-2009 book, not the full twenty-six years.
  • Loss is measured against the original loan amount rather than the balance outstanding at default, overstating severity on long-seasoned loans.
  • The data covers approved loans only, with nothing on applicants who were declined, which is the population an access-to-capital argument most needs.
  • The cost stack charges no guaranty fee. A lender pays the SBA an upfront fee on the guaranteed portion and an ongoing servicing fee, together worth roughly 0.4 to 1.0 percentage points a year depending on loan size and vintage. Applying a fee at the upper end of that range would move about 75 of the 237 profiles into the group clearing under neither condition, leaving roughly 162 dependent on the guarantee and 132 clearing under neither. The 472 clearing without a guarantee are unaffected, since the fee applies only where the guarantee is used.
  • Rates in this record were charged in a market where the guarantee already existed. The comparison shows which loans cover cost at the observed rates, and does not establish what lenders would charge or approve in its absence.
